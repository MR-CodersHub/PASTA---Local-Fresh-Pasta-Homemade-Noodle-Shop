How to Salt Pasta Water: The 10g per Litre Science

'Salty like the Mediterranean sea' is a dangerous culinary myth that will ruin your dinner. We calculate the exact salinity curve and explain the classical Italian 10-100-1000 rule.

Sofia Moretti Culinary Chemist · Research Lead
September 14, 2026
4 Min Read
Lab Verified
Figure 1: Coarse Sicilian sea salt hitting water at 100°C. Notice the immediate burst of vigorous nucleation bubbles. Photo: PASTA Melbourne Testing Kitchen

You have heard the romantic adage a hundred times from celebrity chefs: "Make your pasta water as salty as the Mediterranean sea!" If you were to follow that advice literally, your pasta would be completely inedible. Mediterranean sea water contains approximately 3.8% dissolved salts (38 grams of salt per litre). At that salinity, pasta absorbs so much sodium that the human palate detects only acrid bitterness.

Salting pasta water is the only moment during the cooking process when flavor can penetrate into the innermost core of the noodle. Once drained, no amount of salt sprinkled on top will ever season the interior wheat starch. Here is the precise scientific formula used by Italy's culinary academies.

Remember the golden Italian ratio: 10 - 100 - 1000. That is 10 grams of salt for every 100 grams of dry pasta, boiled in 1000 millilitres (1 litre) of water.

1. The 10 - 100 - 1000 Classical Ratio

This mnemonic device has governed Italian home kitchens for generations:

  • 10 Grams of Salt: Corresponds to approximately 1% salinity. At 1%, the osmotic pressure between boiling water and pasta starch is perfectly balanced.
  • 100 Grams of Pasta: Standard single-person portion size.
  • 1,000 Millilitres of Water (1 Litre): Ample volume to prevent water temperature from plummeting when pasta is submerged.

Quick Kitchen Conversion: Tablespoons to Grams

10g of coarse sea salt = 1 rounded teaspoon. If using fine table salt, use only 7g (table salt packs tighter and contains anti-caking agents that taste metallic).

2. When Should You Add The Salt?

There is endless internet debate about whether to add salt to cold water or rolling boiling water. From a thermodynamic standpoint:

Always add salt when the water is already boiling.

Here are the two physical reasons why:

  1. Pitting Corrosion in Cookware: If you drop coarse salt into cold water, the heavy crystals sink directly to the stainless steel or aluminum bottom. In cold water, salt dissolves slowly, creating localized hyper-saline zones that corrode microscopic pits into your expensive stockpots.
  2. Immediate Nucleation: When salt is dropped into already boiling water, it dissolves in 3 seconds and produces rapid nucleation sites that create uniform, violent turbulence—ideal for keeping pasta strands separated.

The Boiling Point Elevation Myth

Many cooks claim salt makes water boil faster. In reality, adding 10g of salt per litre raises the boiling point by a negligible 0.04°C. The difference is undetectable on a stove.

3. Adjusting for Cured Meats & Aged Cheese

While 10g per litre is the baseline for fresh tomato and vegetable sauces, you must calibrate downwards if your finished sauce includes naturally salty cured ingredients:

Sauce Profile Key Ingredients Recommended Salinity Reasoning
Cacio e Pepe / Carbonara Pecorino Romano DOP + Guanciale 6g to 7g per Litre Pecorino Romano is heavily salted; high water salinity makes the sauce harsh.
Bolognese Ragù Braised beef chuck, milk, wine 10g per Litre Deep meat ragù needs well-seasoned pasta core for contrast.
Burro e Salvia (Butter & Sage) Unsalted butter, parmigiano 10g to 11g per Litre Minimalist butter sauces rely entirely on pasta seasoning.
Seafood Vongole / Bottarga Fresh clams, sea salt, roe 5g per Litre Clam liquor naturally releases concentrated seawater into the pan.
