The Anatomy of Ravioli: Sealing Without Trapping Destructive Air

Why do ravioli burst open in boiling water? It is rarely due to thin dough—it is trapped air expanding like a balloon. Here is the gentle cup-and-press thumb technique passed down by Italian grandmothers.

Lucia Bianchi Lead Sfoglina · Handcrafted Pasta
August 30, 2026
5 Min Read
Emilian Craft
Figure 1: Expelling air from around ricotta mounds using cupped thumbs before cutting with a brass fluted wheel. Photo: PASTA Melbourne Production Table

There is no tragedy in the kitchen quite like lowering a batch of plump, handmade ricotta ravioli into a simmering stockpot, only to watch three of them detonate thirty seconds later, spilling their precious cheese filling into cloudy water and leaving you with empty dough rags.

Home cooks invariably blame themselves for rolling the dough too thin or not pressing hard enough with a fork. In reality, the culprit is basic gas thermodynamics: Charles's Law. When air is trapped inside an airtight pocket and subjected to 100°C water, the gas rapidly expands. If that expanding air has no escape route, the hydraulic pressure blows the weakest dough seam apart.

A raviolo should fit its filling like a tailored silk glove, not a puffy duvet. If you can see an air bubble under the dough sheet, that raviolo is already dead in the water.

1. The Cup-and-Press Thumb Technique

Italian sfogline (master pasta rollers) never use their fingertips to press directly on top of the filling mound. Doing so displaces the filling outward onto the seam line, creating greasy fat barriers where dough can never bond. Instead, they use the sides of their thumbs in a horseshoe shape:

1

Drape the Top Sheet Gently Without Stretching

Lay the top sfoglia sheet over the piped filling mounds like a loose blanket. Never stretch the sheet tight; stretched dough has tension and will pull away from the base as soon as it heats up.

2

The Back-to-Front Horseshoe Sweep

Form a curve with your thumb and index finger around the back of the filling mound. Gently cup down and press the air forward and out the open front edge before sealing the perimeter.

3

Firm Molecular Bonding of the Edges

Once all air is expelled, use the fleshy pad of your thumbs to press down with firm pressure around the perimeter. You are physically fusing the gluten matrices of the top and bottom sheets together.

2. Water vs. Egg Wash: The Bonding Myth

Should you brush your pasta sheet with water or beaten egg wash before sealing?

If your pasta dough was made and rolled properly, you need neither.

Freshly rolled sfoglia has enough ambient tackiness to self-fuse upon contact. However, if your kitchen air is dry and the dough sheet has developed a slight dry skin, brushing with a water-drenched pastry brush is a recipe for disaster: excess water forms a slick barrier that prevents bonding and causes the seam to slide apart.

The Damp Cloth Technique

Instead of brushing water directly on dough, dampen a clean kitchen towel with warm water, wring it out until barely moist, and press it against your palm. Lightly tap your damp palm across the dough sheet for micro-misting without soggy pools.

3. Water Dynamics: Rolling Boil vs. Gentle Simmer

Stuffed pasta must never be subjected to a violent, rolling boil. Violent bubbles toss ravioli against each other and against the hard metal walls of the pot, causing blunt-force seam trauma.

  • Bring water to a rolling boil to dissolve your salt (6g–8g per litre).
  • Drop the heat to a gentle simmer (92°C–95°C) right before dropping in your ravioli. Tiny champagne bubbles rising smoothly to the surface are the ideal cooking environment.
  • Cook time: 3.5 to 4 minutes for fresh; 5 to 6 minutes for frozen. Remove individually with a spider strainer (schiumarola)—never dump filled pasta into a colander!
